What is 1D cutting optimization?

The math that turns a rebar cutting list into the fewest 12 m bars — explained with a worked example you can check by hand.

Explainer · 6 min read · 1D cutting stock / bin packing

The problem in one paragraph

You have stock bars of fixed length (say 12 m) and a list of pieces to cut: 5.5 m, 4.2 m, 3.1 m, 2.0 m… 1D cutting optimization decides which pieces share each bar so that you buy the fewest bars and throw away the least offcut. It is the one-dimensional version of the classic cutting stock problem (also called bin packing): one dimension matters — length. Nesting rectangles on a sheet of plywood is the 2D cousin; cutting rebar, pipes, timber or profiles is 1D.

Worked example — check it by hand

Cut list: 2× 5.5 m, 3× 4.2 m, 4× 3.1 m, 5× 2.0 m (= 46.0 m of steel). Stock: 12 m bars.

Bar 1 — 5.5 + 4.2 + 2.0 = 11.7 m
Bar 2 — 5.5 + 4.2 + 2.0 = 11.7 m
Bar 3 — 4.2 + 3.1 + 2.0 + 2.0 = 11.3 m
Bar 4 — 3.1 + 3.1 + 3.1 + 2.0 = 11.3 m
5.5 m 4.2 m 3.1 m 2.0 m waste
Result: 4 bars, 48.0 m bought, 2.0 m wasted = 4.2% waste. Count the pieces yourself — every cut from the list appears exactly once, and no bar exceeds 12 m. A fifth bar would be pure waste.

How much does it save?

Benchmarks estimators actually use:

  • Up to 10% waste when bars are cut and bent on site without planning.
  • 3–5% — the allowance most contractors add when ordering rebar.
  • 1–3% — what an optimized cutting plan routinely achieves.
  • Below 1% — possible with precut/bent bars and offcut reuse.

Lap splicing alone adds another 5–15% to total steel quantity — which is why laps and cutting belong in the same plan.

The algorithms, briefly

1

Greedy: FFD / BFD

Sort cuts longest-first, then put each into the first (or fullest) bar it fits. Near-optimal in milliseconds — good enough for most lists.

2

Exact: branch-and-bound

Searches the combinations systematically and proves the optimum. Slower, but guarantees no better plan exists for typical rebar lists.

3

The details that matter

Kerf (2–4 mm lost per saw cut), grip/trims at bar ends, and offcut reuse across projects — this is where real tools beat textbook algorithms.

Choosing stock lengths is half the battle

Optimization assumes the stock length; the choice of stock length is a decision of its own. Twelve-metre bars are standard, but if your cutting list clusters around 5.9 m pieces, 6 m stock can beat 12 m stock on waste — at a different price per tonne.
